## 2. Answers to the six questions

**Q1. Which index?** NBR-based indices beat NDVI where compared directly: 91.38% vs 75.86% (P3, cropland, Haryana) and dNBR best among five indices at 90.7% (P2, thesis). The caveat is P4: plain dNBR scored only 41.67-47.44% against fire points in a heterogeneous Indian landscape. **Add NBR / dNBR. Keep NDVI as a secondary signal.**

**Q2. What size of change?** dNBR bands from P1: low 0.10-0.269, moderate-low 0.27-0.439, moderate-high 0.44-0.659, high 0.66-1.300. **I did not find a published NDVI-drop threshold for dry deciduous forest.** I found one NDVI-anomaly method (P13, z-score against a per-date baseline) but no cut-off. The config uses a z-score and flags the number as unsupported.

**Q3. Recovery?** **No Indian dry deciduous study I retrieved gave a recovery time in its abstract.** P14 says deciduous broadleaf forest recovers slowly relative to other cover types; P15 estimates recovery days but the number is in the full text; a review lists Sal at 1-3 years (second-hand). Config uses 12 months as a floor, flagged unsupported, to be tested at 12/24/36.

**Q4. Drought measure and window?** Short windows relate best to fire: SPI/SPEI 3 and 6 (P11), and correlations weaken as the window lengthens (P10). Both are non-Indian. **Use SPI-3, test SPI-1 and SPI-6.** We have only CHIRPS rainfall, so SPEI (needs temperature/PET) is not available in the current pipeline. Drought convention SPI < −1 (McKee et al. 1993, as cited in an AMS preprint).

**Q5. What precedes a fire?**
- **Dry season rainfall deficit**: low Nov-Dec rainfall raised fire-day probability the following dry season (P6). That is a lead time of roughly 3-4 months.
- **Low daily relative humidity and high daily temperature** raised fire-day probability (P6).
- **More dry days and higher maximum temperature** correlate with fire counts across India (r = 0.75 and 0.76, P7).
- **Humidity and actual evapotranspiration** were the top ignition factors at Similipal (P8).
- **Low precipitation, soil moisture and NDVI in Feb-Jun** preceded high-fire months in central India (P9).
- **Gap:** I found no published "N consecutive dry days" cut-off. The config makes it a testable parameter instead of quoting a made-up figure.

**Q6. Leaf-fall vs damage?** Compare each date against its own seasonal baseline, not against the previous month: per-date historical distribution with a probability filter (P12, probability >= 0.9), or NDVI difference divided by that date's standard deviation (P13). Both say seasonal change is not an anomaly.

## 3. One-page summary

**Indicators.** For forest I recommend an NBR anomaly alongside NDVI anomaly, FIRMS fire count near the site, SPI-3, consecutive dry days, temperature anomaly and cumulative loss. Direct comparisons favour NBR over NDVI for burn detection (91.38% vs 75.86% in one Indian study, and dNBR best of five indices in a thesis), so the NBR indicator is switched on, though one Indian comparison (P4) found plain dNBR much weaker, which is why I keep NDVI. I also recommend adding relative humidity: it ranked among the top predictors at Similipal itself (P8) and was a significant variable in the Mudumalai fire-day model (P6).

**What counts as disturbed.** I propose a two-part rule. First, a dNBR of **0.27 or more** (moderate-low severity and above, P1) between a site's own pre-event baseline and the post-event image. Second, a NDVI z-score below a negative cut-off against the per-date baseline to catch non-fire loss. The dNBR number is published; the NDVI cut-off is **not**, and I have marked it for sensitivity testing. The sensor-transfer result (P5) means the 0.27 value should be tested across roughly 0.17-0.37 rather than treated as exact.

**What the literature cannot do for us.** (1) It gives no validated NDVI-drop threshold for Indian dry deciduous forest. (2) Recovery time for this forest type is not pinned down, so the flag duration is a judgement. (3) Weights in a risk score are never published; the literature only supports the *ordering* (fire and dryness signals matter, direction of each indicator), so every weight is an estimate. (4) Drought-fire relationships come mostly from Europe and the USA, and P11 notes human-ignited fires follow drought weakly, which matters because most Indian forest fires are anthropogenic. (5) Optical data cannot see through monsoon cloud, and thresholds do not transfer between sensors or sites (P5). (6) FIRMS counts are detections, not burned area, so they say a fire occurred, not how much carbon was lost.
